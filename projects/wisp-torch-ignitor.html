Section 01

Project Overview & Context

Reliable ignition for liquid and hybrid rocket systems through physics-grounded torch igniter engineering.

In liquid propellant rocket engines, reliable main-chamber ignition is a safety-critical milestone. Hard starts caused by accumulated unignited propellant mixtures can produce catastrophic overpressures that destroy thrust chambers. A spark-ignited torch igniter acts as a miniature, self-contained rocket engine that generates a steady, high-enthalpy flame torch directed into the main combustion chamber to guarantee rapid, repeatable ignition.

Through the Wisconsin Space Program (WISP) at UW–Madison, our team undertook the detailed design, modeling, and analytical evaluation of a gaseous oxygen and propane (GOX/C3H8) torch igniter. Unlike regenerative rocket engines that cycle cryogenic propellants through cooling jackets, this compact igniter employs an uncooled transient heat-sink design, relying on the thermal inertia and high thermal conductivity of a thick-walled metallic liner to absorb peak convective heat fluxes during a target 6.0-second burn cycle.

Collaborative Context & Team Structure
This project is a collaborative initiative developed within the Wisconsin Space Program (WISP). The broader effort encompasses mechanical packaging, plumbing interfaces, ignition electronics, and test-stand operations. This case study and data presentation focus specifically on the analytical modeling suite: evaluating 1D transient conduction, sizing injector orifices, auditing chamber geometric scaling, and synthesizing shifting-equilibrium combustion gas dynamics.

Engineering Objectives & Design Constraints

Defining operational boundaries, mass flow budgets, and thermal safety margins.

The igniter design had to balance contradictory constraints: generating sufficient thermal output to ignite the main chamber plume while preventing heat-sink burnout, acoustic instabilities, and injector phase changes.

Section 03

Data Analysis & Analytical Methodology

First-principles thermal and fluid dynamics combined with NASA CEA chemical equilibrium solvers.

Rather than relying on empirical guesswork, the engineering analysis was formulated around rigorous physical governing equations across four distinct analytical domains:

  1. 1D Radial Heat Conduction: Formulated using Fourier's Law in cylindrical coordinates to solve the transient heat conduction equation ∂T/∂t = α ∇2T, capturing thermal wave penetration and post-burn soakback across candidate metals.
  2. Convective Boundary Modeling: Evaluated using the classic Bartz Equation to determine the local gas-side heat transfer coefficient hg near the throat, combined with Newton's Law of Cooling q″ = hg(Taw - Tw).
  3. Injector Orifice Sizing: Sized via compressible flow relations for gaseous oxygen and gaseous propane, and compared against incompressible square-root orifice relations (ΔP = ½ ρ v2) for liquid single-point injection (SPI).
  4. Thermochemical Equilibrium: Evaluated using NASA's Chemical Equilibrium with Applications (CEA) code through the CEARUN interface to compute chamber temperature, gas molecular weight, specific heat ratio γ, shifting species mass fractions, and nozzle Mach expansion profiles.

Section 04

Thermal Analysis & Material Selection

Bartz heat transfer modeling and transient heat-sink survivability comparison.

The igniter throat experiences the most hostile thermal environment, where peak convective gas velocity and high recovery temperature drive heat flux into the inner wall. To evaluate safe test firing durations, the transient thermal response of three common engineering alloys was analyzed across a 6.0-second firing followed by post-burn soakback (OD = 38.1 mm, Throat ID = 5.89 mm).

Transient Heat-Sink Safe Firing Duration Summary (Throat ID = 5.89 mm, Body OD = 38.1 mm, Test Burn = 6.0 s)
Material Candidate Max Inner Temp (Tinner) Soakback Outer Temp (Touter) Thermal Softening Limit Time to Reach Limit Safety Evaluation
C110 Copper (ETP) 639.9 K 561.9 K 800 K > 6.0 s PASS (Safe for Full 6.0 s Test)
304 Stainless Steel 1297.4 K 399.4 K 1150 K 2.28 s FAIL (Throat Melting Risk @ 2.28 s)
6061-T6 Aluminum 796.9 K 637.2 K 650 K 2.86 s FAIL (Softening / Yield @ 2.86 s)

Temporal temperature traces for inner and outer walls. C110 Copper inner wall peaks at 639.9 K and rapidly diffuses heat radially outward, equilibrating at 561.9 K. In contrast, 304 Stainless Steel's poor thermal conductivity causes severe heat stagnation at the gas boundary, causing Tinner to spike to 1297.4 K.

Spatial gradient from inner throat radius (r = 2.95 mm) to outer casing (r = 19.05 mm) at t = 0.5 s, 1.5 s, 3.0 s, 6.0 s, and 12.0 s (soakback). The copper substrate maintains the entire wall well below the 800 K softening threshold throughout the burn.

Figure 03

Parametric study illustrating that C110 Copper achieves > 10 seconds of safe firing duration once radial wall thickness exceeds 12.5 mm, providing substantial thermal safety margin beyond the 6.0-second baseline burn.

Engineering Takeaway: Dual-Material Heat-Sink Architecture
Inner Core: High thermal conductivity C110 Copper acts as the heat-sink liner to draw heat rapidly away from the combustion gas boundary.
Outer Shell: Low thermal conductivity 304 Stainless Steel forms the structural jacket, retaining structural yield strength under pressure while insulating external mounting brackets from extreme soakback temperatures.

Section 05

Injector Sizing Analysis

Compressible and incompressible orifice sizing, pressure drop optimization, and phase control.

The injector manifold feeds propellants into the chamber at a nominal manifold pressure of 110.0 PSIA with a design pressure drop of ΔP = 20.0 PSID (22.2% Pc) and discharge coefficient Cd = 0.65. Sizing the orifice diameter correctly is vital to ensure steady mass flow, prevent combustion acoustic chugging, and avoid fuel orifice clogging.

Injector Orifice Sizing Summary (Manifold = 110.0 PSIA | ΔP = 20.0 PSID [22.2% Pc] | Cd = 0.65)
Propellant & Phase Mass Flow (mdot) Flow Regime Total Area (Atot) d (N=1 Hole) d (N=2 Holes) d (N=4 Holes)
Gaseous O2 (GOX) 6.00 g/s Unchoked Compressible 6.26 mm2 2.823 mm 1.996 mm 1.412 mm
Gaseous C3H8 4.00 g/s Unchoked Compressible 3.65 mm2 2.155 mm 1.524 mm 1.078 mm
Liquid C3H8 (SPI Reference) 4.00 g/s Incompressible 0.53 mm2 0.820 mm 0.580 mm 0.410 mm
Figure 04

Injector Orifice Diameter vs. Pressure Drop

Parametric curves of orifice drill diameter as a function of feed pressure drop (% of Pc). The designated operating point at 20 PSID (22.2% Pc) establishes reliable acoustic decoupling while utilizing standard drill sizes (1.412 mm for 4-hole GOX, 1.524 mm for 2-hole gaseous propane).

Phase Management: Why Liquid Propane Was Rejected
As shown in the sizing comparison, injecting propane in liquid form requires an extremely small total orifice area of only 0.53 mm2 (a tiny 0.41 mm drill for a 4-hole pattern). In small-scale rocketry, sub-millimeter orifices are highly susceptible to particulate fouling, varnish clogging, and erratic flash-vaporization chamber pops. Operating propane in the gaseous phase enlarges the required orifice area nearly 7x (to 3.65 mm2), ensuring robust fluid flow, reliable spark ignition, and zero orifice clogging risk.

Chamber Geometry & Flow Simulation

Auditing geometric scaling formulas and optimizing characteristic length for flame stabilization.

Chamber geometry governs the residence time available for propellants to atomize, mix, and ignite. During the analytical review of earlier design calculations, our audit identified critical dimensional errors in preliminary spreadsheets that would have severely undermined igniter operation.

Section 07

Results & Aerodynamic Interpretation

Supersonic plume acceleration, convective heat flux peaks, and shifting chemical composition.

Simulation via NASA CEARUN shifting equilibrium revealed key aerodynamic and chemical dynamics governing the torch igniter's plume output:

  • Plume Performance: Total igniter thrust reaches 19.29 N with an exhaust velocity exceeding 1850 m/s and a total thermal energy release of 50.2 kW.
  • Peak Convective Heat Flux: Local heat flux calculated via the Bartz formulation peaks at 7.2 MW/m2 directly upstream of the throat (x ≈ -1.0 mm). This verified our thermal conduction findings that only high-conductivity C110 Copper could absorb this intensity without exceeding softening limits.
  • Combustion Chemical Kinetics: Shifting chemical equilibrium calculations reveal that due to our fuel-rich mixture (φ = 2.42), the exhaust is dominated by carbon monoxide (~70.5% CO) and steam (~16.0% H2O), with subordinate fractions of CO2 (~7.8%) and H2 (~9.2%). This composition ensures high gas enthalpy while keeping combustion temperature manageable for heat-sink survivability.
